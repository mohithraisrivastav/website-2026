Essays from the practice

Essay · 30 May 2026

One of the First Things I Do

One of the first things I do when I arrive at a project is leave all my equipment in one corner and walk around.

I look at the styling. I check what has changed since the recce. I open Sun Seeker on my phone and see where the sun will be over the next few hours. Then I start adjusting the plan.

By the time I reach a project, I already know quite a bit. I have studied the drawings, seen the renders, spoken to the architect, and sometimes the interior designer as well. If I am in the same city, I do a recce before the shoot. I walk the space, check the light at different times of day, decide on angles, and work out the pre production.

By shoot day, I usually have a clear plan of what I want to do and where.

The building then proceeds to change most of it.

What I observe during the recce shifts once the actual shoot begins. The light is different. A space that looked straightforward now needs more time. A room I had not planned around becomes the most interesting one on the day.

I remember photographing a villa where I spent most of the day waiting for one room.

The room had a stone floor, timber furniture, and a large aperture facing the garden. It looked good when I first walked in, but the light was still somewhere else in the house. I photographed other spaces and kept coming back every hour or so.

By evening, the stone floor picked up a warmth that was not visible earlier. The timber looked richer. The room felt larger than it had all day. Even the furniture seemed more comfortable, which makes no sense, but anyone who works with space knows exactly what I mean.

Then there was a project that went the other way entirely.

The weather was bad and we had no option to reschedule, so we went anyway. I reached the property under dark clouds and flat light and started working with what we had.

Then the sky changed.

The clouds shifted and the entire house turned golden.

I had about two hours before it was gone.

Most of my projects take multiple days. I move through a building carefully, wait for the right conditions in each space, and come back if I need to. Two hours for a full property is not how I prefer to work.

But for those two hours, the laterite walls looked darker, the timber picked up warmth, and light bouncing off the pool started reaching parts of the house that had been flat all morning.

A house in June feels different from the same house in December. A courtyard on a cloudy day is a different space from that same courtyard on a clear winter morning. A bedroom facing east tells a different story at sunrise than it does at noon.

When I studied architecture, most of my attention was on plans, sections, elevations, materials, and details.

Now I spend most of my time inside finished buildings.

The drawing can tell me where an aperture is. It cannot tell me that a patch of reflected light from a neighbouring wall will become the most interesting thing in the room for twenty minutes every afternoon. It cannot tell me that a corner nobody talked about during design discussions will become the place everyone gravitates towards.

Those things reveal themselves over time.

Once a building is finished, the weather changes, the seasons pass, the furniture gets moved, and people start making the place their own.

The drawings stay the same.

The building does not.
